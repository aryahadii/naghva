# Artist writing

An artist's article is a biography: the lead at the top of their page and the
Life & work sections below it. Write it the way a good Wikipedia biography is
written: the story of a musical life, told in order, in plain and neutral prose
that a reader can follow from the first line to the last. Every sentence rests
on evidence, but the article is about the artist, not about the evidence.

This guide covers how articles read. For what may be claimed, follow the [data
policies](data-policies.md); for who gets a page, the
[definitions](definitions.md), not Wikipedia's notability rules. For Wikipedia's
own advice, see its guides to [the lead section][wp-lead],
[biographies][wp-bio], [words to watch][wp-words] and [writing better
articles][wp-better].

## The reader

Write for one person: a curious listener who has never heard of the artist,
reads the page in their own language and won't open the sources. They need to
know who each person is, where and when things happened, and how one thing led
to the next. Give them those premises in full sentences. Cut repetition, but
never the words that connect one fact to another.

## Story and record

The page around the article already holds the record. At a glance gives the
basics, Recordings shows every release with its full credits and dates, Events
lists the announced performances, and Sources keeps every quote. The article
doesn't repeat those lists. It tells what they can't: how the artist came to
music, what they set out to do, who they made it with, what changed and why.

The record's [life events](data-framework.md#life-events) are the story's raw
material: single moments in the sources' own words and languages. The article
is where they become a story. It chooses the moments that matter, puts them in
order, joins them with the links the sources give, and tells them in plain
Persian and English. A life event never appears in the article as a quote
because it is there; quote only when the person's own words say more than a
retelling would.

So the article selects. A recording, a credit or a concert belongs in it when it
is a step in the story: a first song, a breakthrough, a new sound or line-up, a
collaboration that lasted, a ban, a return, a last work. Everything else stays
in the record, where readers still find it. Many findings belong there and
nowhere else.

A story is real events in order, joined by the links the sources give. Don't add
motives, feelings or turning points that no source states, and don't fit every
Iranian musician into the same story of exile, censorship or tradition meeting
modernity. Facts you know but can't cite go back to research as leads.

Start from the story. Before drafting, tell the artist's life in a few plain
sentences from the life events and other checked evidence, without naming any
source. Then choose the facts that tell it, write for the reader, and only then
attach the citations.

## Shape

**Lead.** The text before the first heading is the article in miniature. Its
first sentence says who the artist is: their name, what they do or did, where
they come from and when they were active. The next sentences say what they are
known for and give the main steps of their career, so that a reader who stops
there knows the essentials. The lead is also the page's search description. Keep
it to one or two short paragraphs, without lists of works.

**Sections.** Tell the life in order, one period per section. Headings are short
and plain, and name a time, a place, a group or a turning point: "Early life",
"Black Cats", "After the revolution", "Later years". The page outline lists
them, so they should read well as a list. A section on songwriting, voice or
style works when sources discuss it. Avoid clever titles and lists of things,
such as "Film, installation and orchestra".

**Paragraphs.** Each paragraph moves the story on or explains one thing. Open
with the point, often a time or a change, rather than a release title. Link
sentences with time and cause (then, after, by then, because, so, until) where
the sources support the link. When "also", "too" and "alongside" start to pile
up, the paragraph has lost its thread.

**Links.** Link the first mention of a recording to its panel, and of a person
or group to their page when they have one. People without a page stay plain
text.

**Length.** Let the evidence set it. A short page that reads well is better than
a long one that lists. Don't pad, and don't apologize for what is missing;
research gaps stay in capture.

## Sentences

### Readable sentences

Psycholinguists link a handful of sentence habits to slower reading, and current
language models overuse them ([Clark, Martin and Mahowald, 2026][clark]). They
are common in Naghva's drafts, in both languages.

- **People are the subjects.** In a story people act; documents don't. Write
  "Rian began working professionally in 2017, when he joined Jetpack as a singer
  and songwriter", not "His biography dates the beginning of his professional
  work with Jetpack to 2017". Biographies, catalogues, platforms and releases
  shouldn't describe, place, list or preserve anything in the prose.
- **Plain verbs.** "Golsa wrote the song" reads faster than "the song credits
  Golsa as songwriter". Verbs made from nouns, such as credit, bill, date, list
  and feature, are record-keeping words.
- **No noun stacks.** Three nouns in a row make the reader rebuild the phrase:
  "The dispute with Caltex" is easier than "Caltex catalog dispute".
- **Related words close together.** Don't make the reader carry a subject
  through a long aside before its verb arrives; split the sentence instead. Keep
  optional words such as "that" and "who" when they help.
- **Common wording.** "She blends jazz, soul and Iranian sounds" reads faster
  than "Her work brings jazz and soul into conversation with Iranian pop". An
  unusual phrase has to earn its place.
- **Premises.** Introduce people when they first appear ("the composer Esfandiar
  Monfaredzadeh") and say where and when things happen. A shorter sentence isn't
  clearer if the reader has to guess what it left out.

### Evidence in the prose

- **State facts plainly.** Write the sentence for the reader, then attach every
  reference it needs; a run of citations shows the reader one number per source.
  An artist's own account of plain facts, such as where they were born or whom
  they studied with, can be stated directly.
- **Name a source only when it changes the meaning:** a memory ("he later
  said"), an opinion ("a Radio Zamaneh critic heard it as close to art rock"),
  or accounts that disagree about something that matters. Do it once, lightly.
  "In an account reproduced by Bahari, its lyricist Ardalan Sarfaraz traced its
  inspiration to reading Kafka…" becomes "Ardalan Sarfaraz wrote the lyric after
  reading Kafka…".
- **Small disagreements don't need a sentence.** When sources differ on a detail
  such as a year, write what both allow and cite both: "in 1969 or 1970". Say
  who says what only when the difference changes the story: who wrote a song,
  what it was about, whether a concert took place. The record keeps both
  versions either way.
- **Data notes stay in the record.** Editions, platform dates, archival release
  dates, separate products and which credit applies to which track are the
  record's business. Delete sentences such as "These credits apply to the named
  tracks" or "The performance and its much later archival publication are
  separate dates".
- **Bookings are bookings.** An announcement supports a booking, not a
  performance. Mention one when it matters to the story and put the difference
  in the verb: "Later that year she was booked to perform in Paris and Berlin."
  Events lists the rest. A report or a recording of the night lets you say that
  it happened.
- **Collaborators, not credit lists.** Name the people who matter to the story
  and leave full roles to the Recordings panel. "With production, mixing and
  mastering by Mehrdad Mcy and electric guitar by Mazaheri" is a credit block,
  not a sentence.
- **Dates the reader can use.** In English, give Gregorian years; a Solar Hijri
  year becomes the span it covers (1372 SH is 1993–94) unless the month settles
  it. In Persian, prefer Solar Hijri dates, and keep a Gregorian date as the
  source gives it when converting would blur it. Give the exact day only when
  the day matters.
- **Neutral, not flat.** No praise or ranking in Naghva's own words; let facts
  and named sources carry the weight. A short quotation of the artist can say
  more than a paraphrase. In the other language, translate it; the citation
  keeps the original.
- **Living artists.** Include private life only when it shaped the music and a
  reliable source reports it.

## Persian and English

The two pages tell the same story with the same facts, order and uncertainty,
each written the way a good writer of that language would write it. Draft each
from the evidence and compare them afterwards; translating sentence by sentence
carries one draft's awkwardness into the other. Follow the [name
policy](data-policies.md#names) in both.

**Persian.** Write plain written Persian, the Persian of a good book or
newspaper, not of an official letter or a translation.

- Let people act: «آرین در سال ۲۰۱۷ با پیوستن به جت‌پک کار حرفه‌ای‌اش را آغاز
  کرد», not «زندگی‌نامهٔ شخصی او آغاز فعالیت حرفه‌ای‌اش با جت‌پک را سال ۲۰۱۷
  می‌داند». Don't make a document the subject of «می‌داند»، «قرار می‌دهد» or
  «ثبت کرده است», and turn «نام او در … آمده است» into what the person did.
- Use simple verbs such as «خواند»، «نواخت»، «ساخت»، «نوشت» and «اجرا کرد»
  instead of «… را بر عهده داشت»، «به اجرای کنسرت پرداخت»، «حضور یافت» or «مورد
  استفاده قرار گرفت».
- Keep the verb within reach. Persian puts it last, so a long sentence keeps the
  reader waiting; split it rather than piling clauses before the verb, and break
  up long ezafe chains.
- Drop the fillers of translated Persian: «نیز» and «همچنین» in sentence after
  sentence, «به‌عنوان» and «در قالب» where a plain verb works, and «می‌توان به …
  اشاره کرد».
- Write Persian names and titles in Persian script wherever the record has them,
  with titles in «».

**English.** Use Gregorian years. Set albums, EPs and films in italics and songs
in quotation marks, in the English forms the record gives.

## Example

Two paragraphs from Farhad Mehrad's page. Both versions cite the same passages.
The rewrite follows the events in order, makes people the subjects and turns a
sentence about two sources' dates into "1969 or 1970". It also drops a dispute
that the sources don't contain: Monfaredzadeh's and Farhad's accounts of "Jomeh"
agree.

**Before**

> “Mard-e Tanha,” written by Shahyar Ghanbari with music by Esfandiar
> Monfaredzadeh, was made for Masoud Kimiai’s *Reza Motori*. Farhad initially
> doubted that his voice suited Persian songs. Monfaredzadeh offered to erase
> the recording if he disliked it; Farhad recorded with a live orchestra and was
> satisfied with the result. The composer’s chronology places this work in the
> summer of 1349 SH (1970), while the estate biography gives 1348
> SH.[^bbc-mard][^monf-mard][^mard-estate]
>
> “Jomeh” brought Ghanbari, Monfaredzadeh and Farhad together again for Amir
> Naderi’s *Khodahafez Rafiq*. Its connection with the Siahkal uprising needs a
> distinction between the collaborators’ accounts: Monfaredzadeh said the event
> motivated him, but that he had not told Ghanbari or Farhad this when asking
> them to work on the song. Farhad also said that the subject had not been
> discussed between them. In 1357 SH, Monfaredzadeh made a new arrangement for
> the events of 17 Shahrivar, titled “Jomeh Baraye
> Jomeh.”[^monf-jomeh][^bbc-jomeh][^monf-jomeh2]

**After**

> In 1969 or 1970 the composer Esfandiar Monfaredzadeh, who had heard Farhad
> sing at Couchini, asked him to sing for Masoud Kimiai’s film *Reza Motori*.
> Farhad was reluctant: he thought his voice did not suit Persian songs.
> Monfaredzadeh promised to erase the tape and find another singer if he
> disliked the result. Farhad recorded “Mard-e Tanha,” with words by Shahyar
> Ghanbari, in the studio with a live orchestra, and was pleased with
> it.[^mard-estate][^monf-mard][^bbc-mard]
>
> In the winter of 1970–71 the same three made “Jomeh” for Amir Naderi’s film
> *Khodahafez Rafiq*.[^monf-jomeh] Monfaredzadeh later said he had written it
> for the Siahkal uprising but kept that to himself. He asked Ghanbari only for
> a song about the gloom of a Friday evening, and asked Farhad to sing it.
> Farhad said they never spoke about it, though the mood of the time and
> Monfaredzadeh’s leanings had given him some guesses.[^bbc-jomeh] Monfaredzadeh
> returned to the song for the Friday of 17 Shahrivar 1357 (8 September 1978),
> rearranging it and recording it again as “Jomeh Baraye Jomeh.”[^monf-jomeh2]

## Checks

Authors run these before submitting. Reviewers run them again and propose
rewrites, not only flags.

1. Read the article aloud from the lead to the end. It should sound like one
   person telling another about an artist's life.
2. Skip the Recordings and Events panels. Can you still follow the story?
3. Look at the subject of every sentence. Rewrite those whose subject is a
   source, a platform or a listing.
4. Delete sentences that explain the data rather than the life.
5. Find paragraphs that walk through releases, credits or bookings one by one.
   Keep the ones that matter and say why they matter.
6. Put another artist's name into a paragraph. If it still reads true, it says
   nothing about this artist.
7. Check each sentence against its citations. A rewrite must not add a claim for
   flow or lose a qualification that matters.
8. Compare the two languages: same facts, names, dates and uncertainty.
