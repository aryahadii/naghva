Kourosh Yaghmaei, born in 1946, is an Iranian rock singer, guitarist and composer.[^born][^first-work] His 1973 hit “Gole Yakh” was a turning point for him and for Iranian rock.[^brothers][^gole-yakh-year] After the 1979 revolution he spent at least 25 years barred from working, yet he chose to stay in Iran.[^banned-25][^stay] Reissues by Now-Again Records brought his 1970s songs to listeners abroad, and the same label released his later albums.[^brink][^reissues][^now-again][^rebel-date]

## Early life

Yaghmaei was born into a well-off family; one distant ancestor was a popular poet and his grandfather was a wealthy landowner.[^born] He began music at ten on a santur his father gave him, and at fifteen he took up the guitar, which had always been his favourite instrument.[^santur][^guitar15] As a teenager he started a band with friends. They called themselves The Raptures and played songs by their favourite garage rock and British Invasion bands, learned from imported records by groups such as The Ventures.[^raptures] He went on to lead his own rock bands as bandleader and lead guitarist, and later formed a band with his brothers.[^guitar15][^brothers] He named the Ventures, the Beatles, the Rolling Stones and Bob Dylan as his main inspirations.[^influences]

## “Gole Yakh”

In the final year of his sociology degree, Yaghmaei made his first professional work, “Gole Yakh,” composing, arranging, playing and singing it himself.[^first-work] The song, about a love that lasts through the bitterest winters, became a hit in 1973, with his brother Kamran on electric guitar.[^brothers][^kamran] The American rapper Nas later built his song “Adam and Eve” on its melody.[^nas]

## Silence after the revolution

When the new government came to power in 1979, Yaghmaei was barred from releasing records, travelling and performing live. He was ordered to report again and again to the new Revolutionary Courts, and for the first several years also to Evin prison.[^barred][^evin] His voice was kept off the air for 17 years from 1979–80, and for that whole period he was also banned from showing his face and from leaving the country.[^ban][^first-ban]

He made his living from children’s stories, published as books and tapes, from [*Diar*](#دیار-5pm), and later from teaching guitar.[^livelihood] *Diar* was an album without vocals: ten Iranian regional melodies arranged for the first time for a large modern orchestra.[^diar-bio][^diar-detail] His brother Kambiz moved to Europe, but Yaghmaei stayed, though teaching guitar put him at risk of arrest.[^son][^stay] “I believed that if I changed my career, it would be an obvious insult to music and to myself and ignoring my cultural roots,” he said later.[^stay-quote] In a 2013 letter he added that anyone who leaves the homeland is labelled a fugitive for ever.[^leaving]

## Return

Yaghmaei returned in 1994–95 with [*Sib-e Noghreii*](#سیب-نقره-ای-ftf), his first album with vocals in seventeen years.[^return][^first-vocal] The government allowed the release, but he was forbidden to put his face on the cover.[^permission][^cover] It includes “Raz-e Del,” a nine-part set of six songs, two chaharmezrabs and a tasnif for large and electronic orchestras, choir and traditional Iranian instruments.[^raz] More albums followed, among them [*Maho Palang*](#ماه-و-پلنگ-ce4), [*Kaboos*](#کابوس-jk2) and [*Arayesh-e Khorshid*](#آرایش-خورشید-n7m), on which his son [Kaveh Yaghmaei](/en/kaveh-yaghmaei-cba/) played keyboards on two songs.[^later-albums][^son][^arayesh-track]

## *Malek Jamshid* and the second ban

Yaghmaei recorded [*Malek Jamshid*](#malek-jamshid-q7e) between 2003 and 2006 in an ordinary room of his apartment, without acoustic treatment, a sound engineer or professional microphones.[^malek-recording][^malek-room] The work took two to three years on a small budget, with the title song alone taking at least a year.[^malek-two-years][^malek-duration] The album is named after a king of Persian myth.[^malek-name] It passed every stage of censorship and received a permit, but a second ban on his work then stopped its release; by his 2013 letter that ban had lasted nine years, and he had still been given no answer.[^permit-stopped][^second-ban]

Meanwhile his old songs found new listeners abroad. Now-Again Records released *[Back From the Brink](#back-from-the-brink-ah9): Pre-Revolution Psychedelic Rock From Iran: 1973–1979* in 2011, and Now-Again and Finders Keepers reissued many of his songs for record collectors and fans of psychedelic rock.[^brink][^reissues] In 2011 he was running a private music school and studio in Tehran.[^school] Now-Again finally released *Malek Jamshid* on 10 June 2016, without an Iranian permit.[^malek-release][^malek-two-years][^now-again]

## Later work

The single [“Baroon Barooneh”](#baroon-barooneh-gcn) came out in May 2023, and [“You Weren’t Here”](#you-werent-here-ca2) in January 2024.[^baroon-date][^ywh-date] [*Rebel*](#rebel-qx3), a twelve-song album, was released worldwide by Now-Again on 9 February 2024; its title song was the album’s second [single](#rebel-single-vsd).[^rebel-date][^rebel-product][^rebel-single] In 2025 Nick AM remixed his song “Leila,” and Dochar released a new [“Gole Yakh”](#gole-yakh-acm) featuring him.[^leila-name][^leila-date][^dochar-name][^dochar-date]

In December 2025 Yaghmaei announced that his last album, *Pelak 44*, would soon be released by Now-Again, and that after it he would step away from music.[^farewell-album][^farewell-quit]

## Rights dispute

In a statement on his Telegram channel, Yaghmaei said he had never granted anyone the rights to two of his albums: *Gole Yakh*, and “Kourosh Yaghmaei’s New Concert,” recorded with great difficulty in the first years after the revolution. Even so, a company was selling the second under a new title, [*Parandehe Mohajer*](#parandehe-mohajer-s48), with a different insert photo and two instrumental tracks removed.[^caltex-dispute]
