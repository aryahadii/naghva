Farhad Mehrad (1944–2002) was a singer, guitarist and pianist from Tehran. He began by performing Western songs and joined Black Cats in the 1960s, before recording Persian songs such as “Mard-e Tanha” and “Jomeh.” He chose poetry that expressed his own beliefs and paid close attention to how listeners heard its words.[^birth][^death][^blackcats][^studio-b][^poetry][^book-diction]

After the 1979 revolution, Farhad was barred from continuing his public work. He kept making music at home, eventually returning with [*Khab Dar Bidari*](#khab-dar-bidari-nst) and [*Barf*](#barf-a3m), albums in which he set poetry to music. His wife, Pooran Golfam, helped him obtain permits and arrange performances, and later shared recordings he had left behind.[^restriction][^khab-permission][^khab][^barf][^bbc-pooran][^archive-sketches]

## Early life

Farhad was born in Tehran in 1944. As a small child, he listened outside the room where his brother and his friends practised violin. His family bought him a cello, but the lessons lasted only a few sessions. At school he wanted to study literature; his uncle insisted on natural sciences instead, and Farhad left in the eleventh grade.[^birth][^childhood][^school]

After leaving school, he learned to play by ear with a group of Armenian musicians and became their guitarist. On a trip south to perform at an oil-company club, the bandleader asked him to stand in for the absent singer. Farhad went on to sing in Italian, French and English, taking particular care with pronunciation. In 1963–64 he appeared on the television variety programme *Studio B*, then sang with his guitar at a concert at Tehran’s Amjadieh stadium.[^learning][^pronunciation][^television]

## Black Cats

In 1966–67 Farhad joined Black Cats at Tehran’s Couchini club, singing and playing guitar and piano. The band’s trumpeter, Manouchehr Eslami, remembered that Farhad could not read music but needed only to hum a song a few times to fit his voice and playing to the group. He still came to rehearsals out of respect for the other musicians.[^blackcats][^eslami]

During these years he sang “Age Ye Jo Shans Dashtim” for the Persian dub of *My Fair Lady*. Later, when interviewers asked about his first Persian song, he did not mention it. He left Black Cats to visit his sister in England, where a planned two-month stay stretched to more than a year. He performed in London cafés, but did not pursue an English producer’s proposal to make an album.[^bbc-dubbing][^london]

## Persian songs

In 1969 or 1970, the composer Esfandiar Monfaredzadeh, who had heard Farhad at Couchini, asked him to sing for Masoud Kimiai’s film *Reza Motori*. Farhad was reluctant: he thought his voice did not suit Persian songs. Monfaredzadeh promised to erase the tape and find another singer if he disliked the result. Farhad recorded “Mard-e Tanha,” with words by Shahyar Ghanbari, with a live orchestra in the studio, and was pleased with it.[^mard-estate][^monf-mard][^bbc-mard]

In the winter of 1970–71, the three made “Jomeh” for Amir Naderi’s film *Khodahafez Rafiq*. Monfaredzadeh later said he had written it for the Siahkal uprising but had kept that to himself. He asked Ghanbari for a song about the gloom of a Friday evening and asked Farhad to sing it. Farhad said the mood of the time and Monfaredzadeh’s leanings had given him some guesses, but they never spoke about it. Monfaredzadeh rearranged the song for the events of 8 September 1978 (17 Shahrivar), calling it “Jomeh Baraye Jomeh.”[^monf-jomeh][^bbc-jomeh][^monf-jomeh2]

Farhad sang relatively few Persian songs. He wanted to believe in the poetry and feel that it expressed his own state of mind. “Ayeneha,” one of these early songs, began with Ardalan Sarfaraz’s repeated readings of Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* in Sadegh Hedayat’s translation. Sarfaraz named the lyric “Maskh,” after the book, and dedicated it to both writers.[^poetry][^ayeneha-kafka]

His work with Monfaredzadeh continued through the 1970s. They made “Koodakaneh,” also known as “Booy-e Eydi,” with Ghanbari’s words about Nowruz. Monfaredzadeh rearranged the old children’s rhyme “Gonjeshkak Ashi Mashi” for Farhad after using Pari Zanganeh’s version in *Gavaznha*. “Saghf,” with words by Iraj Jannati Ataei, was used in *Mahiha Dar Khak Mimirand*, and “Shabaneh 2” was finally recorded in late summer 1978.[^monf-koodakaneh][^koodakaneh-alias][^koodakaneh-nowruz][^gonjeshk-original][^monf-gonjeshk][^monf-saghf][^monf-shabaneh2]

## After the revolution

In the first days after the 1979 revolution, television broadcast Farhad singing “Vahdat,” a poem by Siavash Kasraei set to music by Monfaredzadeh. Yet Farhad was subsequently barred from continuing his work, and repeated requests to reissue his recordings were refused. Golfam dealt with much of the work of seeking permits and arranging performances while he continued composing at home.[^monf-vahdat][^vahdat-broadcast][^vahdat-bbc-date][^restriction][^bbc-pooran][^khab-permission]

He returned to concert audiences in Vienna in 1992–93. There, as well as older songs, he performed three pieces he had not yet released: “Kooch-e Banafsheha,” “Khial-e Khoshi” and “Khab Dar Bidari.” The [recording of the concert](#live-in-vienna-1993-56r) survives.[^vienna]

In 1993–94 he finally obtained permission for *Khab Dar Bidari*, his first album released in Iran after the revolution. He combined older Persian songs and English covers with new music for poetry by Mehdi Akhavan-Sales, Mohammad Reza Shafiei Kadkani, Juan Ramón Jiménez and William Shakespeare.[^khab-permission][^khab]

He changed the texts as he set them to music. For “To Ra Doost Daram,” he selected lines from Akhavan-Sales and added lines of his own. He made Jiménez’s poem into a new lyric for “Khab Dar Bidari” and reworked Kadkani’s “Kooch-e Banafsheha” to fit his melody. His concern for words extended to singing them: he said he wanted listeners to understand every word on the first hearing, whether he sang in Persian or English.[^book-to-ra][^book-tora-poem][^book-khab][^book-kooch][^book-diction]

His return to a Tehran stage came at Cinema Sepideh in December 1994. Officials forbade advertising, printed tickets, photography and filming, and told him not to sing “Gonjeshkak Ashi Mashi.” The audience filled the hall, and he sang it anyway.[^bbc-sepideh][^sepideh-book-date]

## Barf and later years

Farhad’s next album again ran into permit refusals. During a concert trip to the United States in 1997–98, he recorded and released *Barf* there; it was permitted in Iran the following year. He composed most of its pieces, drawing on poets including Nima Yushij, Abusa’id Abolkhair, Esmail Khoi and Fereydoun Rahnema.[^barf][^barf-iran-date][^barf-title-poem]

He wanted an orchestral recording but could not afford it. Andranik used synthesizers and sampled sounds in the arrangements. Other pieces had simpler accompaniment: Farhad sang “Gol-e Yakh,” his version of “Edelweiss,” in Persian and English with his own guitar.[^barf-arrangement][^book-edelweiss]

The poems reached him in different ways. Golfam recalled that he named “Katibeh” after first seeing Rahnema’s poem on the poet’s gravestone. For “Vaghti Ke Bacheh Boodam,” he substantially reshaped Khoi’s text, cutting passages and rewriting others. In late 1998, at Tehran’s Hotel Shahr, he performed “Katibeh” and “Gandhi” in a concert that was [recorded](#live-in-tehran-1998-wep).[^book-katibeh][^book-khoei][^tehran]

He next planned *Amin*, an album of songs from different countries and languages, but illness prevented him from completing it. After treatment in Iran and France, he died in Paris on 31 August 2002 and was buried at Thiais.[^amin][^death]

## After his death

Golfam initially hesitated to release the sketches Farhad had recorded while practising at home, knowing how much he cared about sound quality. She later decided to share them. They became part of the five-CD [*Farhad 159*](#مجموعه-159-فرهاد-pab), issued in 2003–04, which brought together Persian and non-Persian recordings. It ends with “Banooy-e Gisoo Hanaei,” a home recording of Farhad’s adaptation of Nâzım Hikmet’s poem in Ahmad Puri’s translation.[^archive-sketches][^archive-dates][^archive-discs][^archive-presentation][^book-banoo]

His family had kept other recordings too. His sister Nahid had once sent him a list of favourite songs and asked him to record them for her in London. She kept the cassette and gave it to Golfam in 2008–09. A limited release was halted over sound quality.[^nahid][^sweet]

In 2012–13 Golfam opened “Khaneh-ye Farhad” at Tehran’s Cinema Museum, making his personal belongings available to visitors. The songs Nahid had kept eventually appeared as [*Sweet as You Are*](#sweet-as-you-are-cjq) in 2025–26.[^museum][^sweet]

More archive recordings followed. [*Farhad in Black Cats: Studio B Show*](#farhad-in-black-cats-studio-b-show-ns6), recorded in the 1960s, came out in 1401 (2022–23), and [*Echoes of My Mind*](#echoes-of-my-mind-zyz), recorded in 1363 (1984–85), in 1402 (2023–24). Three volumes of *The Covers Collection*, [*Youth Echoes*](#the-covers-collection-i-youth-echoes-nk6), [*Whispers at Home*](#the-covers-collection-ii-whispers-at-home-gkk) and [*Studio Sketches*](#the-covers-collection-iii-studio-sketches-et2), were released in 2025.[^studiob][^echoes][^covers1][^covers2][^covers3]
