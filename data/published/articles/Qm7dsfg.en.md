Azam Ali is an Iranian-born singer, composer and producer who grew up in India. She co-founded the duo Vas in 1996 and has made solo albums alongside her work with the group [Niyaz](/en/niyaz-9bb/).[^formation][^titles][^solo][^niyaz] Her solo albums range from early-music repertoire and lullabies to her own electronic productions.[^portals-purpose][^lullabies][^phantoms-making]

## From santur to singing

Azam grew up in an English boarding school in India from the age of four. She moved to the United States with her mother in 1985 and began studying the santur. Her teacher was Manouchehr Sadeghi.[^formation][^migration][^early] She initially wanted to become an instrumentalist. While learning passages that required singing and playing together, her teacher encouraged her to develop her voice; a singing teacher at college later encouraged her too.[^singing] She preferred the santur’s rougher folk sound to the piano-like approach she heard in Persian classical playing.[^santur]

In Vas, which she formed with percussionist Greg Ellis, she often sang without words.[^vas][^early] She connected that choice to growing up among different languages: she could respond to a song without understanding its words, and felt that words could not always express her feelings. In a 2002 interview, she said that only one piece across the group’s first three albums used Rumi’s poetry; the rest was wordless.[^early]

## The first solo albums and Niyaz

Azam had sung in an early-music choir and studied that repertoire before making [*Portals of Grace*](#portals-of-grace-xpq), released in 2002.[^early-music][^portals-date] She used the album to explore connections between medieval European music and music of the Arab world.[^portals-purpose] She produced the album and arranged most of the music, with Ellis contributing percussion, keyboards, vocal drones and string arrangements. Oud, cello and other acoustic instruments accompanied the voices.[^portals-players][^portals-production]

On [*Elysium for the Brave*](#elysium-for-the-brave-ha9) (2006), she sang predominantly in English for the first time, including lyrics she had written herself.[^english][^elysium-date] She worked with producer and keyboardist Carmen Rizzo, and with musicians including guitarist Trey Gunn and drummer Pat Mastelotto.[^elysium-production] Rizzo and multi-instrumentalist [Loga Ramin Torkian](/en/loga-ramin-torkian-ce3/) were also her partners in Niyaz. On that group’s second album, *Nine Heavens* (2008), they combined electronics with Persian folk songs and Sufi poetry; Torkian drew, in particular, on Amir Khusro’s verse and old Iranian folk melodies.[^niyaz][^khusro]

## Lullabies and electronic production

After her son was born, Azam began collecting lullabies and writing some for him, without initially planning an album.[^lullabies] That work became [*From Night to the Edge of Day*](#from-night-to-the-edge-of-day-8ff) (2011).[^night-date] She wanted to include minority regions: for “Noor” she fitted Tajik Persian words to a melody she had in mind. The wordless “Tenderness” grew from the first tune she sang to him.[^noor][^tenderness] Turkish lullabies, words in the Sirjan dialect of Iran and Sorani Kurdish texts sat alongside one another, accompanied by Torkian’s lutes, Rizzo’s drones and the Bozzini String Quartet.[^night-languages][^night-ensemble]

Azam described some of her best songs as discoveries made while experimenting freely in the studio, which she then refined and shaped.[^experiments] For [*Phantoms*](#phantoms-zd2) (2019), she took on singing, programming and production herself. Torkian played guitars, Gabriel Ethier added synthesizers and programming, and Leah Metzler played cello; Michael Patterson mixed the album.[^phantoms-making][^phantoms] She drew on the 1980s and 1990s music of 4AD and Wax Trax, and especially Depeche Mode, whose synthesizers had first made her want to create music with technology. She described her lyrics as rooted in her own experiences and philosophical outlook.[^influences][^lyrics]

## Later work

In 2022, Azam performed with Torkian and an acoustic ensemble at the Getty Villa, where images were projected onto the museum facade for *Persia After Hours*. She later recalled the performance as one of her most cherished stage experiences.[^getty-date][^getty][^getty-memory]

She had written a follow-up to *Phantoms* but was unsure she would release it when her friend Christian, the owner of COP International, offered to fund it if she finished it.[^christian][^offer] She released [*Synesthesia*](#synesthesia-hrf) in November 2025, producing it herself and again working with Torkian on guitars and Patterson on the mix.[^synesthesia]

That December, she worked with Efrén López on [“Once More, All Is Green”](#once-more-all-is-green-ena), a track based on Hila Sedighi’s Persian poem about Iran’s political and social struggles. Azam sang and produced the track; López played several instruments and contributed production.[^poem][^joint] In September 2026, she and Torkian released Niyaz’s [*Seven Realms of Attar*](#seven-realms-of-attar-jkk), the musical foundation for a theatrical work inspired by Attar’s *Conference of the Birds*. They produced the album together, and Azam contributed voice, percussion, keyboards and programming.[^attar][^attar-credits]
