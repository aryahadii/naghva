Hichkas is the stage name of Soroush Lashkari, an Iranian rapper born in Tehran on 9 May 1985.[^born] Often called the godfather of Iranian rap, he founded one of Iran’s first rap groups, 021, and in 2005 or 2006 released [*Jangale Asphalt*](#jangale-asphalt-3aq), a debut that set hip-hop beats against classical Iranian instruments.[^godfather][^021][^debut-sound][^debut-channel][^debut-catalogue] He left Iran in 2010, soon after releasing [“A Good Day Will Come”](#a-good-day-will-come-sf3), and has lived in London since.[^ye-rooze][^route] From there he released his second album, [*Mojaz*](#mojaz-mes), in 2020, and a series of protest songs.[^mojaz-meta][^bezan-plane]

## Early life

Lashkari lived in Germany until he was two, then grew up in the Vanak neighbourhood of Tehran.[^germany] He studied English at university but dropped out to make music.[^dropout] Around 2003 he began rapping at Persian freestyle sessions in Vanak.[^vanak] He chose the name Hichkas, “nobody,” for the contrast with the impact he wanted to have: “Nobody stands for nothing, and I like that,” he later said.[^name]

In 2003 or 2004 he founded 021, one of Iran’s first rap groups, under the slogan “Tehran forever.”[^021] He turned Tehran’s calling code into songs and into a hand sign that became a symbol of Iranian hip-hop and its followers.[^tehran-code] He and his friends shot grainy videos on the streets of Tehran at night, walking ahead of a reversing car to be lit by its tail-lights. Because hip-hop was largely illegal, he sold his work in secret, taking orders by email and calling buyers first to screen out undercover agents and then delivering the CDs to their doors.[^grainy][^covert]

## *Jangale Asphalt*

*Jangale Asphalt* (*Asphalt Jungle*) came out in 2005 or 2006 and fused hip-hop rhythms with classical instruments such as the santur.[^debut-channel][^debut-catalogue][^debut-sound] His longtime producer, [Mahdyar Aghajani](/en/mahdyar-aghajani-92j/), had classical training and set out to build a distinctly Iranian sound with instruments such as the tar and the ney, while Hichkas wrote his lyrics in Persian rather than English.[^collaboration][^aghajani-sound] Its hit “Ekhtelaf” (“Disparity”) pictured the capital as a place that “tempts you till it saps your soul.”[^ekhtelaf]

His lyrics drew on devices from classical Persian poetry but spoke in everyday street language.[^language] He avoided swearing and songs about violence, drugs and women, and wrote instead about addiction, depression, poverty and youth unemployment. “We feel we shouldn’t have bad influences on people,” he said.[^clean] He dressed like any young man in the street, and his songs spoke of national pride and an old-fashioned code of honour.[^honour] In the 2008 video for “Ye Mosht Sarbaz” (“A Bunch of Soldiers”), he stands with his crew beside Iranian flags.[^sarbaz]

The album had no permit from the Ministry of Islamic Guidance.[^court] In 2006 he and his friends were arrested while selling copies on the streets of Tehran.[^jangale-arrest][^aj-arrest] “I remember the excitement and anxiety we had when hand delivering first copies of the album in secret which eventually led to my arrest,” he wrote later.[^debut-memory] In 2008 a court summoned him on a charge of promoting and publishing music without a licence. He was released on bail of five million tomans, on a promise not to rap without a licence until his trial.[^court] In these years he also recorded [“Bazam Kalan”](#bazam-kalan-htg) with Reza Pishro and [“Vagheyitar Az Mostanad”](#vagheyitar-az-mostanad-q28), and appeared on Amir Tataloo’s [“Tamas Karish Nakon”](#tamas-karish-nakon-bgj).[^baz-name][^baz-date][^vag-date][^tam-name][^tam-date] He also appeared in Bahman Ghobadi’s 2009 film *No One Knows About Persian Cats*.[^persian-cats]

## “A Good Day Will Come” and exile

After the disputed 2009 presidential election, Hichkas released “Ye Rooze Khoob Miad” (“A Good Day Will Come”) in February or March 2010, a song heard as his response to the crackdown.[^good-day-fa][^ye-rooze] It was his last recording made inside Iran, and it came out as his plane was leaving Mehrabad Airport.[^exile][^flight][^mehrabad] Its title is still written as graffiti on walls in Tehran.[^flight] According to testimony in Nahid Siamdoust’s book *Soundtrack of the Revolution*, the authorities had nudged him to leave.[^mehrabad] He went first to Malaysia, then to Turkey, and settled in London.[^route][^england]

In London he went on recording, and in October 2012 released “Young n’ Foolish” with Quf, Reveal and Kool G Rap.[^young] His single “Firooz” came out on Nowruz 2015.[^firooz] He kept working on *Mojaz*: in 2014 he played extracts of an unfinished song for it, describing an album that would keep its Iranian roots while making “a more complex music rather than the typical hip-hop.”[^identity][^collaboration] He finished recording it in March 2018, opened preorders in March 2020, and released it that year with 16 tracks; one of its songs, [“Rosva”](#rosva-vam), has his words and Mahdyar’s music.[^recording][^recording-date][^preorder][^preorder-date][^mojaz-meta][^rosva] Censorship runs through his work. He once asked what would have happened if Hafez, Rumi and Muhammad had needed a government permit to publish.[^hafez] In a 2018 Telegram post on “sanctioned music and musicians,” he wrote that sanctions are a way of putting a person, a group or a country under pressure, and can take many forms.[^sanction]

## Protest songs

On 10 December 2019 Hichkas released [“Dastasho Mosht Karde”](#dastasho-mosht-karde-vv4) (“They’ve Clenched Their Fists”) to remember the November protests; it had more than 200,000 plays on SoundCloud in three days.[^rf-release][^rf-plays] Closer to spoken word than rap, over a setar melody by Mahdyar, it speaks of the urban poor who are called “the enemy” and shot in the streets, and ends with recordings of protesters under fire.[^bbc-spoken][^dastasho-telegram][^rf-poor] Its cover listed the names of people killed in the crackdown in red on black, with “first and last name unknown” repeated among them.[^rf-cover]

In 2021 he released [“Bezan”](#bezan-zn5) in protest at the Revolutionary Guards’ downing of a Ukrainian plane.[^bezan-date][^bezan-plane] Many listeners thought he had retired, but during the Woman, Life, Freedom uprising of 2022 he released [“Inyekiam Vase”](#inyekiam-vase-pp6) (“This One Is For”), a hip-hop spin-off of Shervin Hajipour’s “Baraye.”[^retired][^bezan-plane] Where his 2010 song had hoped that a good day would come, it promised: “We will be bringing a good day.” It had millions of plays on SoundCloud.[^bringing][^millions] In October 2022 he reported a suspicious man near his home in London; the police made his home a security priority, and such incidents have since limited his freedom of movement.[^threat][^police]

Since 2023 he has released a run of singles. He wrote, composed and produced [“Ma Edame Darim”](#ma-edame-darim-yfd); [“Tigham Rooye Raget”](#tigham-rooye-raget-2sp) has music by Urf, and he shared the words of [“Har Ye Rooz”](#har-ye-rooz-s2e) with Poobon, who composed it.[^ma-edame-credits][^ma-edame-producer][^tigham-credits][^har-date][^har-credits] [“Dishab Ye Basiji Koshtam”](#dishab-ye-basiji-koshtam-pxs) came out on 9 May 2024, and [“Rooye Jenazat Miraghsam”](#rooye-jenazat-miraghsam-gpk) on 24 May 2024, less than a week after the crash of Ebrahim Raisi’s helicopter.[^basiji-date][^jenazat] He also appeared on Kudd’s [“Barobax”](#barobax-cdm) in August 2024, and released [“Hanoo Be Yadetam”](#hanoo-be-yadetam-63p) on 17 September 2026.[^bar-name][^bar-date][^han-date]
