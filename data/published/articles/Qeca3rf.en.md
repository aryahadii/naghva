Arash Sobhani is an Iranian singer, guitarist and songwriter who founded [Kiosk](/en/kiosk-tgn/) in Tehran in 2003. His songs bring colloquial Persian and humor into rock music. After moving to the United States in 2005, he continued working with Kiosk and later made recordings with Andy Fite and under his own name.[^roles][^kiosk][^speech][^humor][^stone][^dood]

## Early life

Arash was born in Tehran and spent part of his adolescence in Northern California. He inherited records by Pink Floyd, the Eagles and Supertramp from his uncle, and began playing guitar at fourteen while in the United States. He returned to Iran in 1986.[^childhood][^uncle][^guitar][^return] Hearing Bob Dylan changed what rock music meant to him: it could express his thoughts about life and society.[^dylan][^expression]

He studied architecture in Iran while pursuing music, which he had been doing underground since high school.[^architecture] By 2003, he had played electric and acoustic guitar with [Raz-e Shab](/en/raz-e-shab-8xp/) on [*Dar-E-Qali*](#dar-e-qali-the-rug-weavers-loom-phb).[^raz][^raz-date]

## Kiosk and songwriting

Arash later traced Kiosk’s beginnings to a change in how he wrote. Trying to obtain permission for songs had led him to censor himself, so he decided to write without imagining a censorship committee’s response. That project grew into Kiosk.[^censor][^freedom] He had written songs for other singers until Babak Khiavchi, a guitarist in the band, heard his demos and encouraged him to sing them himself. Kiosk’s first album, [*Ordinary Man*](/en/kiosk-tgn/#ordinary-man-adam-e-mamooli-jh7), came out in 2005, the year Arash moved to the United States.[^demos][^babak][^ordinary-date][^roles]

Writing Persian for guitar and bass was a central concern for him. He drew on speech heard on streets and buses, and described humor as a way to get a point across by making a listener laugh.[^farsi][^speech][^humor] His songs could be private conversations with himself, too. In 2014 he compared writing them to keeping a notebook: sometimes to remember something, sometimes to get it out of his mind. He preferred not to identify which songs were personal, saying that doing so would change his relationship with them.[^notebook][^private-songs]

## Arrangements and stories

By 2012, Arash’s growing affinity with the mixture of joy and sadness he heard in Roma music had led Kiosk to add violin. Tara Kamangar, a pianist who joined the band on violin, brought that instrument into the group.[^violin][^tara] For [*Call a Cab (Zang Bezan Azhans)*](/en/kiosk-tgn/#call-a-cab-zang-bezan-azhans-2ab) in 2014, the musicians changed their approach to arranging. Rather than shaping every song collectively, they entrusted each to an individual arranger, including Ardalan Payvar and Mohammad Talani.[^arrange]

Remembering the people who had shaped his listening led Arash back to Kami Tull, a seller of bootleg recordings who had introduced him to Creedence Clearwater Revival and J. J. Cale. Kiosk made [*Stereo Tull Presents*](/en/kiosk-tgn/#stereo-tull-presents-rty), a concept album about him, in 2016.[^cale][^taste][^tull][^tull-date]

For [*Sweet Destiny*](/en/kiosk-tgn/#sweet-destiny-pjc) in 2021, Arash began with old photographs and a story. He and his collaborators then invited visual artists to help turn it into a work combining animation and live action. They gave the artists freedom to use a different technique for each song. For Arash, an album could encompass a chapter of his life and thoughts.[^sweet-date][^photos][^story][^visuals][^chapter]

## Later recordings

In 2023, Arash and the composer Andy Fite released [*Stone Soup*](#stone-soup-ep-n5j), a jazz EP of six live recordings.[^stone][^stone-live] His solo album [*DOOD*](#dood-g7y) followed in 2025. Working with composers from different countries on the television and music project *New York Harmony* helped shape the album. He recorded it in New York, Toronto and Lund.[^dood][^harmony][^studio]
