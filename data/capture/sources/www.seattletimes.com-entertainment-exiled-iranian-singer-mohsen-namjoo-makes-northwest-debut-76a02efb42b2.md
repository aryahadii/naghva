> Namjoo and Abdi began performing with their rock band, Maad, in 2002, but the authorities shut them down.

> Drafted, he met his most important influence in the army, the guitarist who goes by the name Abdi, who introduced Namjoo to the blues.

> A few days before the concert, the government canceled the show.
Malaysia’s communications and culture minister Dr. Rais Yatim said the 33-year-old singer-songwriter was “not appropriate in terms of religion, culture and the country’s cosmopolitan nature.”

> Namjoo currently is living in exile in California, because a rock song he wrote and recorded, “Shams,” included a quotation from the Koran.

> “I’m not religious,” he said last week by telephone from Palo Alto, where he is studying music at Stanford University. “But religious people were insulted. A person who sings the Koran on TV — all the religious people have a good connection with the government — brought my CD to the court and the judge gave me a five-year sentence.”

> “I miss my family,” said the singer-songwriter, who has not been home since 2007. “I miss my good friends. Most important thing is my mother. I miss her. She’s so old. She can’t come anywhere.”

> he fell out with the music department at the University of Tehran over what he called its “rigid and backward” views.

> The spirit of Robert Johnson and John Lee Hooker reminded him of Iranian village music.

> In 2007, Namjoo released his debut album as a leader, “Toranj,” which set the classic visionary poetry of Hafez, Rumi and others to the driving style of Muddy Waters and Stevie Ray Vaughan.

> “Dah e Shast,” by contrast, deals in a more gentle, but determined folk-rock manner with the “troubles” of the ’80s, when the exhilaration of the revolution of 1979 turned dark, with executions and the triumph of theocracy.

> Namjoo is proud to be in such company but cautions that “the music is more important in my mind than politics.”
